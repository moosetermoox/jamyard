# Game Coherence Review — 2026-08-02

41 built-in games reviewed by 14 parallel agents for coherence and classroom
worthwhileness (not technical correctness — sims cover that). Each game was
mentally played as teacher and as student, judged against PEDAGOGY.md and
COMPARATIVE-ADVANTAGE.md.

**Verdicts: 7 feature-worthy · 11 fine · 19 needs-work · 4 cut**

Pattern analysis lives in the session summary / NEXT-STEPS; per-game notes below,
sorted by worth-it score. Verdict key: feature-worthy (front-page good), fine
(solid library keeper), needs-work (good bones, named fixes), cut (not worth
class time even fixed).

## both-sides-rope — FEATURE-WORTHY — worth 5/5 — ~18min
This is the thesis in one activity: the class's own evidence becomes the rope, and the before/after stance charts let the room literally watch its own mind move — no competitor can run any part of this loop. The AI step earns its place (sorting, merging, naming the point of tension — interpretation, not decoration), and 'Changed your mind? That's thinking, not losing' is the rare end screen that lands.
  + The delta reveal is a genuine shared-fate payoff: two bar charts of the same class, minutes apart, with the movement itself as the lesson — a process trace, exactly what the comparative-advantage doc calls documentation rather than display
  + The AI instruction is unusually well-crafted: uses the YES:/NO: prefixes but judges unprefixed answers by content (14-year-olds WILL forget the prefix), keeps every distinct idea, explicitly forbids declaring a winner, and ends on the strongest tension — a built-in discussion hook
  + Stance options include 'Lean yes/Lean no/Not sure' and the prompt says 'It's okay not to be sure' — low-floor, dignity-protecting, and it makes small mind-movements visible in the delta instead of only full flips
  + Well-chosen default claim (homework) that every student has a stake in, with the end screen correctly framing the whole thing as a swappable template for history/science/novels
  [MAJOR] whatif (collect) → stance2 (collect-choice): students write what-if questions but NOBODY sees anyone else's before re-voting — the what-ifs only surface as a list in the final delta reveal, after the second vote is already cast. The arc claims these questions 'pull the rope,' but structurally they can't move a single mind. Fix: insert a reveal (or a quick AI pick of the 3 sharpest what-ifs) between whatif and stance2, so the re-vote is actually informed by them.
  [MINOR] delta (reveal): {{whatif.responses.list}} dumps up to ~25 raw what-if questions onto the projector directly under the payoff bar charts, burying the game's best moment in a wall of text. Cap it, or route it through a summarize step that keeps the 3-5 questions 'still pulling at the rope.'
  [MINOR] rope-reveal (reveal): with 25 students the merged rope can be 15+ bullet lines with no guidance on how long to sit here — this is the game's central read-aloud/discussion beat, but the screen doesn't say so. One line of teacher-facing framing in the template ('Read each side aloud — which pull surprises you?') would keep a first-time teacher from clicking past it in 20 seconds.

## one-more-thing — FEATURE-WORTHY — worth 5/5 — ~16min
This is the thesis in one activity: addressed (you inherit a specific classmate's list), chained (each step's output is literally the next step's input box), and a double payoff — the personal 'your list came back grown' moment followed by the AI-built collective memory — layered on top of legitimately evidence-backed retrieval practice. No competitor can run any part of the middle, and the arc lands twice; the one real hole is that inherited lines are freely editable, so one vandal can hand a classmate back a gutted list.
  + Retrieval-first design ('from memory — no notes!') is the strongest pedagogy in the library: the testing effect plus peer elaboration, and every add is low-floor (one line)
  + The returned reveal (scope:own) is a genuine only-Lanyard moment — your own work comes back improved by two specific classmates, which is belonging and review at once
  + The AI cluster step is real work, not decoration: 25 grown lists → one deduplicated, theme-grouped master list, and 'credit the catch, not the student' is exactly the right safety instruction
  + Prompts are specific and staged well: add-one asks for 'a detail, a missing point, or a connection', add-again explicitly says 'already grown once' — students always know where they are in the chain
  + Pacing is tight for what it delivers: ~5.5 min of timed input across four phases, no foreach-over-25-responses drag anywhere
  [MAJOR] add-one / add-again phases: 'leave their lines alone' is enforced only by honor — the inherited list is prefilled into an editable box, so any student can delete or rewrite a classmate's work, and the vandalized result goes straight back to the victim's screen in the 'returned' reveal with no teacher gate in between. That inverts the activity's belonging payoff for exactly the kid it hurts most. Fix: render inherited lines read-only with an append-only input beneath (the queued append-only mechanic), or insert a preview phase before 'returned' so the teacher sees chains first.
  [MINOR] returned phase: the host screen has no guidance for how long to hold here — each student needs ~60-90 quiet seconds to read their grown list, and a teacher who clicks Continue at normal announce speed will steamroll the game's best moment. A hostTemplate line ('Give everyone a minute to read what came back') would protect it.
  [MINOR] recall phase: a dud opening list ('idk') poisons its whole 3-person chain — the two inheritors have nothing to build on. A one-line addition to the add-one prompt ('If the list is empty or thin, start it yourself — write the biggest thing you remember') gives inheritors a rescue move.

## one-voice — FEATURE-WORTHY — worth 5/5 — ~12min
The purest expression of the shared-fate thesis: the whole class succeeds or resets together, a tap is full participation, and the ending reframes every failure as part of the story. Five minutes of play buys a genuine room-changing moment no competitor can structurally offer.
  + Rules announce is excellent teacher-read-aloud copy: three sentences, the collision rule, and 'Just feel the room' — sets tone without over-explaining
  + Lowest possible floor (one tap = full participation) with real collective tension; the quiet student matters exactly as much as the loud one
  + The 'story' reveal is the rare ending that lands: attempts/resets/bestRun turn failure into shared narrative, and 'Nobody won. Everybody did.' is the thesis in five words
  + family:'connection' means scoring is structurally impossible, not just absent — the promise is enforced
  [MINOR] count phase has no maxAttempts/mercy path (the one-voice recipe has this param; this config doesn't). If a 30-student class never reaches 20 and the teacher force-advances, the 'story' reveal still declares 'We counted to {{count.target}} as one voice' — it announces a success that didn't happen. Either add a fallback template branch for the incomplete case or adopt the recipe's maxAttempts so the reveal can honestly say 'our best run was 17.'
  [MINOR] collisionWindowMs is fixed at 400 regardless of class size; target 20 with 30+ tappers is dramatically harder than with 8. The rules announce could set expectations ('big classes restart a lot — that's the game') so early resets read as fun rather than failure.
  [MINOR] story reveal copy nit: '{{count.attempts}} attempt(s)' — the '(s)' hedge reads clumsy on a projector in the game's emotional payoff moment; worth a plural-aware template or rewording ('Attempts: 3').

## someones-got-you — FEATURE-WORTHY — worth 5/5 — ~15min
This is the thesis in miniature: an addressed moment (a specific classmate's real thing lands on YOUR screen and you must respond to it), structurally winner-free, with a teacher gate before anything reaches the projector. The copy has a genuine voice ('Write like someone's day depends on it. It might.'), the arc builds to a payoff that earns its minutes, and no competitor product can run this shape at all.
  + The rotateFrom addressed moment is the whole game and it works: encouragement is assigned, not chosen, so popularity dynamics can't operate — nobody waits to see if anyone picks them
  + Preview gate before the wall honors the 'nothing student-written reaches the projector without a teacher gate' rule exactly where cruelty could sneak in
  + passAllowed on the notes phase protects the student who isn't up for disclosure this week, matching the pedagogy doc's 'Pass counts as full participation' commitment
  + The reveal-one wall is a real payoff: 4-5 minutes of the teacher reading one kind line at a time to the room, with each note's author privately recognizing their own moment
  + Showing only the encouragement line (not the original note) on the wall is a quietly correct privacy call — the vulnerable disclosure stays between the pair
  [MINOR] notes/boost: a student who passes on 'notes' contributes nothing to the rotation, so nobody writes them a line and they get nothing on the wall — which contradicts the card description's explicit promise that 'nobody is missed.' Either soften that copy, or give a writer whose assignment is empty a fallback prompt ('write one line for anyone in this room having a quiet week') so every student still receives something.
  [MINOR] boost: the 120s timer auto-submits whatever is in the box, so half-typed or blank encouragement can land in the preview queue; the teacher gate catches it, but the recipient then gets nothing. Consider a gentle default ('Someone in this room is rooting for you') for empty auto-submits, or passAllowed with the same fallback routing.
  [MINOR] check (preview): rejectNext loops the ENTIRE class back through the boost collect — a heavy hammer when the real remedy for one mean line is Hide. The template text steers toward hiding, but the reject button should be labeled to make clear it restarts the writing round for everyone, so a teacher doesn't hit it to fix one bad entry.

## closer — FEATURE-WORTHY — worth 4/5 — ~35min
This is the thesis made playable: escalating pair disclosure with structural safety (pass renders invisible, reveals are simultaneous and pair-scoped, no scoring possible), original well-sequenced prompts, and an ending — the one-word class checkout followed by 'You just had nine real conversations' — that genuinely lands. Its honest weaknesses are runtime (nine questions at 2-3 minute timers is a full advisory period, not a 15-minute warm-up) and that the config never explicitly tells pairs to talk to each other, which is the difference between nine conversations and nine silent screen-reads.
  + Prompt escalation is expertly judged: window-or-aisle → class mascot → teleport is a true low floor, and tier 3's 'proud of that you rarely get to talk about' / 'thank one person' / 'moment to remember in ten years' earn their longer timers — nothing reads as forced intimacy, and pass is always available
  + Safety is enforced by mechanics, not disclaimers, and the welcome announce says so up front in student language ('You can pass on any question, anytime. Nobody will know.') — exactly the framing PEDAGOGY.md commitment 3 calls for
  + Partner choreography is deliberate: same partner within a tier (reusePairsFrom) so a thread can build, new non-repeat partner between tiers (rotatePairsFrom), triples for odd classes so nobody is benched
  + The checkout is a model ending — the whole class's one-word feelings revealed as a shared list converts eighteen private exchanges into one collective moment, and the end message names what just happened
  [MAJOR] All nine share phases (t1q1-share … t3q3-share): the reveal template shows the two answers and nothing else — no instruction to actually talk. A pair that reads silently and waits for the teacher gets zero conversation out of a conversation game. Add one line to each share template ('Take a minute — tell your partner what stood out') or put the talking norm in each tier intro.
  [MINOR] Whole-game pacing: 22+ minutes of collect timers alone if run to expiry, realistically 30-40 minutes with the talking that is the point. That is fine for advisory/homeroom but the library card should say so, and a teacher has no graceful exit ramp mid-game — consider a short variant (2 questions per tier) or note in the config description that tiers are the natural stopping points. The config has no playTime metadata at all.
  [MINOR] tier1-intro / tier2-intro: 'You have a partner' / 'New partner!' is announced before students see who it is — the partner's identity presumably appears on the collect screen, but the intro should set the expectation ('your partner's name will appear on your screen') so nobody is scanning the room confused in the first ten seconds.
  [MINOR] t1q1 timer: 120 seconds for 'window seat or aisle seat' is double what the warm-up needs — the first question sets the game's tempo, and a room full of done-in-15-seconds students staring at a 2-minute countdown starts the game flat. 60s here (teacher can always close early, but defaults teach).

## finish-my-drawing — FINE — worth 4/5 — ~20min
A genuine exquisite-corpse activity with a real chained/addressed hook — 'nobody knows what theirs became' is a promise no competitor's tool can make, and the teacher preview gate before the projector is exactly right. But the payoff phase undercuts the promise: the gallery drags across 25 sequential reveals and captions only the final artist, so the original starter may never find out what theirs became.
  + Textbook chained moment: rotateFrom passes each drawing through three real classmates, and the suspense framing in the intro sells it honestly
  + Low-floor and safe — anyone can start a doodle, no scores, and the preview phase gives the teacher a gate before anything student-drawn hits the projector
  + Tight, legible arc: draw, pass, pass, reveal — a 14-year-old understands the whole game from the 10-second intro
  [MAJOR] gallery (reveal-one): with 25 students this is 25 host-paced animated reveals — roughly 8-12 minutes where each student only really cares about one drawing. Attention will die by reveal ~10. Fix: reveal a sampled highlight set one-by-one then drop the rest into a grid finale, or split the class-attention cost some other way.
  [MAJOR] gallery (reveal-one): the caption credits only the FINAL artist (item.name on continue-2.responses), so the intro's core promise — 'you won't know what yours became until the gallery' — has no mechanism to land; the starter must visually recognize their opening strokes under two layers of additions. The engine already stores assignedFrom links (chain-reveal.js) — caption each drawing 'Started by A · continued by B · finished by C'.
  [MINOR] start-drawing: 45s with auto-submit means a student who freezes hands a classmate a blank or two-stroke canvas, and that emptiness propagates down the whole chain. Bump the first round to 60-75s, or seed a 'draw literally anything — a shape counts' nudge in the prompt.

## snowball — FEATURE-WORTHY — worth 4/5 — ~12min
The cleanest arc in the library: alone → pair-merge → whole class, with the intro honestly telling students why the solo step exists ('nobody can skip the thinking step') and an ending line that actually lands. The merge phase's agree-to-submit is real shared fate on a question ('What should our class norms be?') where the answers genuinely matter — no competitor can run this, and it earns its 10-12 minutes.
  + Enforced think-before-pair: the 90s solo collect structurally prevents the loud kid from doing the pair's thinking
  + agreeMode 'both' makes the merge a genuine negotiation, not a shared textbox; 240s is the right amount of time to actually argue
  + The end message ('Started as everyone's own idea. Ended as a few strong ones — built by all of us') names the pedagogy back to the class instead of fizzling
  + connection-family enforcement means the norms conversation can never mutate into a scored one
  [MINOR] Tagged 'decide' but nothing decides: the share reveal shows ~12 merged norm lists to a 25-student class and then ends, so the teacher who ran this to actually GET class norms is left doing the convergence by hand. Add a rank or pick-one vote over pairs.merged (vote is not on the connection-family banned list), or use the recipe's snowballAgain second merge into fours to shrink 12 candidates to ~6.
  [MINOR] The hardcoded solo prompt makes this shipped config a first-week-of-school one-shot; the library card copy should say so ('best on day one / after a rough week'), since a teacher discovering it in March will bounce off the norms question.

## weekend-poem — FINE — worth 4/5 — ~8min
The best minutes-to-payoff ratio of the three: five minutes, every student types one sentence, and the class gets a shared artifact made of its own words — a genuine chained moment no competitor can produce. Its one real flaw is arithmetic: a 4-line poem cannot honor 25 inputs, so the 'everyone contributes' promise quietly breaks for most of the room.
  + Lowest possible floor — one sentence about your weekend, 60s timer, no wrong answers — exactly the Monday-morning check-in the recommendedFor copy claims
  + The reveal is a true chained/collective artifact: the class's own words become the payoff, which is the thesis moat in its simplest form and something Kahoot legally cannot even collect
  + Short arc with no filler: collect, process, reveal, done — it never overstays
  [MAJOR] The process phase asks for a 4-line poem that 'combines ALL these weekend activities' — with 25 responses that instruction is impossible, the AI will pick 4-6 and the other ~20 students scan the poem, don't find their weekend, and the belonging payoff inverts into being left out. Either lengthen the poem (one line per 2-3 students, ~8-12 lines for a full class) or change the instruction to honestly weave in as many as fit and name that it's a sampler.
  [MINOR] The reveal phase projects raw AI output with no preview gate — Story Builder routes the identical shape through a teacher preview, and a Monday-morning poem built from unvetted teen free-text is exactly where the teacher wants a 10-second read-first. Insert a preview phase between process and reveal.
  [MINOR] The reveal has no participatory beat — students read silently for 20 seconds and the end phase fires. A one-line host prompt on the reveal ('Whose weekend can you spot?') would turn the payoff into 60 seconds of the class talking, which is the whole point per PEDAGOGY.md commitment 1.

## whose-eyes — FEATURE-WORTHY — worth 4/5 — ~18min
This is the addressed/chained thesis in its cleanest form: the class builds the viewpoint pool, each student steps inside a specific classmate's suggestion, and the payoff is the room hearing itself from 25+ perspectives with no scores anywhere. The arc lands and the ending hands the teacher a real next move ('the question column is tomorrow's lesson'), but the climax currently depends on students correctly retyping their assigned viewpoint, and the ~12 min claim is badly optimistic at full class size.
  + Pure moat activity: rotateFrom delivers an addressed moment (a specific classmate's suggestion, not a pool) and the viewpoints collect chains directly into step-inside and the reveal — no competitor can run this
  + Low-floor first step ('a parent at dinner? the kitchen table it gets done on?') — the example nouns in the viewpoints prompt teach the move better than any instructions could
  + No-score design matches the empathy goal; the reveal-one circle makes collective thinking visible without ranking it
  + End message reframes the output as tomorrow's lesson material and tells the teacher the topic is editable — the activity advertises its own reuse
  [MAJOR] step-inside's first field asks the student to manually copy the assigned viewpoint ('Whose eyes? (copy the viewpoint above)') solely so the circle's {{_current.eyes}} template resolves. Students will mistype, abbreviate, or skip it, and the payoff card renders 'Through the eyes of ' with a hole at its emotional center. Auto-carry the assigned value (prefill the field from {{viewpoints.assigned}}, or resolve the itemTemplate from the assignment link) and drop the busywork field.
  [MINOR] circle (reveal-one) over 25-30 four-field cards read aloud is ~10 minutes by itself, making the stated ~12 min playTime really ~18-20 at full class size. Either fix the playTime claim or give the teacher a sanctioned way to sample (e.g. route through a preview so they can star highlights, or host copy suggesting 'read 8-10, leave the rest on screen').
  [MINOR] No preview gate between step-inside and circle: student-written impersonations (potentially of a named real person — 'a substitute teacher') go straight to the projector. The text filter catches profanity, not mockery-in-character. An optional preview phase before the circle would match the project's own 'teacher gate before class-wide reveal' instinct for high-attribution content.
  [MINOR] intro announce has a 12-second timer but three sentences of setup the teacher needs to read aloud and frame; it will auto-advance mid-sentence. 20-25s, or no timer (host-paced continue), fits how a teacher actually launches this.

## art-gallery — FINE — worth 3/5 — ~15min
A clean, safe draw-and-share template with a real personal moment (your named drawing, animated onto the projector), but the arc is shallow: draw, wait, look, done. It earns its library slot as the drawing on-ramp, yet with 25 students the one-at-a-time gallery is most of the runtime and there is no collective beat to make the viewing active.
  + The preview gate before the gallery is exactly right for student drawings — the teacher sees every thumbnail before the class does, which is the codified safety rule for drawing surfaces
  + Low-floor and genuinely fun prompt ('dream invention') — anyone can scribble something in 90 seconds, and inventions invite laughs without targeting anyone
  + The reveal-one gallery gives every student an addressed moment: their work, their name, its own beat on the projector — not a scrolling grid dump
  [MAJOR] gallery (reveal-one): viewing is entirely passive. With ~25 drawings the class sits through 5-8 minutes of look-at-the-next-one with nothing to do on their Chromebooks. Add one interactive beat — e.g. reveal the drawing before its caption and let the class guess what the invention is aloud, or a quick post-gallery 'which invention should exist?' vote — so the payoff is a class moment, not a slideshow.
  [MINOR] intro (announce): 'No erasing your neighbor's masterpiece' is a joke imported from Finish My Drawing — in this game nobody ever touches anyone else's drawing, so it reads as a rule for a mechanic that doesn't exist and will confuse literal-minded students. Replace with a line that sets up THIS game (e.g. 'every drawing goes up, so give it your best 90 seconds').
  [MINOR] check (preview): rejectNext loops the ENTIRE class back to redraw. The realistic failure is one inappropriate drawing, and the right tool is the moderation hide on the collect phase — but the preview screen offers the teacher a nuclear 'Try again' as the visible alternative. The preview template text should tell the teacher to hide individual drawings from the console instead of rejecting the whole round.
  [MINOR] end: the closing message ('Swap in your own drawing prompt for any topic') is addressed to the teacher, not the room — the class ending fizzles into template documentation. Give students a one-line send-off and move the remix tip to the description/library card where teachers actually read it.

## charades-bowl — NEEDS-WORK — worth 3/5 — ~40min
The core is genuinely good and genuinely Lanyard: the phrase bowl is chained from student free text (which Kahoot legally cannot even collect from under-16s), and Fishbowl's same-pool/three-rounds memory mechanic is faithfully implemented. But the pool scales linearly with class size and nothing caps it — at 25 students the bowl holds 75 phrases and three rounds through it is a 35-45 minute session, not the advertised ~25, with most students spectating most of that time.
  + Student-written phrases becoming the game material is a real chained moment — the class plays with its own words, and hearing your phrase come out of a teammate's mouth in round 3 is a payoff no quiz platform can produce
  + The three-round escalation (describe → act → one word) is the proven Fishbowl arc and the intro explains it cleanly up front; the shrinking-information gimmick works precisely because the pool repeats
  + Honest self-labeling: 'recommendedFor' warns it needs a group comfortable performing, and the classSize field admits the sweet spot is smaller than a full class
  [MAJOR] round1/round2/round3 (turn): the pool is 3 phrases × every player with no cap, so a 25-student class plays three rounds through 75 phrases. At realistic rates (describe ~6/min, act ~3-4/min) that's 30+ minutes of turns alone, during which 23 of 25 students are watching. Cap the pool (e.g. draw ~30 phrases for the round regardless of class size) or scale phrases-per-student down as class size goes up (3 phrases under 10 players, 1 above 20).
  [MAJOR] phases (whole game) vs playTime: '~25 min' is only true near the 6-10 player floor. For the classSize ceiling it advertises (24), the honest number is 35-45 minutes — a teacher who trusts the card will hit the bell mid-round-2 with the leaderboard unreached and the game unresolved. Either fix the pool size (above) or state playTime as a range tied to class size.
  [MINOR] phrases-1/2/3 (collect ×3): three sequential full collect phases with timers (75s+60s+60s) plus two host transitions just to gather three short strings is ~4 minutes of setup drag. The engine's multi-field collect would take all three phrases on one screen in one phase.
  [MINOR] end: 'Great game! Thanks for playing Charades Bowl.' fizzles after a 3-round team battle — the leaderboard auto-advances on a 30s timer into a generic goodbye. Name the moment instead (a winner callout or a 'rematch with the same bowl' suggestion), and consider dropping the leaderboard's auto-advance timer so the teacher controls how long the final standings breathe.

## class-critique — NEEDS-WORK — worth 3/5 — ~10min
The core idea is strong — a rate phase turning the class's collective judgment on a presentation into visible bar/pie charts is exactly the 'documentation, not display' thesis, and no competitor has it. But the shipped config is visibly damaged by test edits and its arc has a hole where the presentation should be, so as-is a teacher who runs it cold gets an embarrassing projector and a fizzled ending.
  + The rate phase with three custom labeled scales (Familiar–Fresh, Tough–Doable, Won't solve it–Nails it) is a genuinely good structured-critique instrument, and the labels are specific rather than generic 1-5s
  + visibility: 'all' means the class sees its own aggregated judgment — collective sense-making per PEDAGOGY.md commitment 1, and something Mentimeter can only do once per deck
  + Short and reusable: as a post-presentation ritual it costs almost nothing and the 'customize the scales' framing in recommendedFor is the right pitch
  [MAJOR] intro phase: hostTemplate is 'happy birthday ' — leftover test data. The projector shows 'happy birthday' instead of the instructions while students read the real message on their devices. Delete the hostTemplate field.
  [MAJOR] intro phase: the message says 'The presenter will share their idea with the class' but the intro auto-advances after 15 seconds straight into a 60-second rating timer — there is no beat for the actual presentation. Remove the intro timer (make it host-paced with a 'Presenter goes now — click when they're done' continue) or add an untimed announce phase for the presentation itself.
  [MAJOR] phase-4 (reveal): has no template or content configured — it is a blank 15-second screen between the rate results and 'Critique complete.' The rate close already renders the charts; either delete phase-4 entirely (rate-it → end) or give it a real discussion prompt like 'Where did we disagree the most? Presenter: which score surprised you?'
  [MINOR] intro phase: the attached image (02hest-kids-superjumbo webp, plus a stray phone photo in assets/) appears to be unrelated test uploads projected to the class. Remove both assets or replace with something on-topic.
  [MINOR] rate-it: 60 seconds to score three scales is fine, but the ending gives the presenter no right of reply — the payoff of a critique is the conversation the charts start. A closing announce that hands the mic back ('Presenter: one thing you'd change based on this?') would make the ending land instead of cutting to 'Critique complete.'

## class-quiz-showdown — NEEDS-WORK — worth 3/5 — ~22min
The concept is real Lanyard: the class writes the quiz about itself and then plays it — a chained, addressed activity Kahoot structurally and legally cannot run, with a genuine 'learn about your classmates' payoff. But the foreach iterates over every single response, so a 25-student class sits through 25 near-identical 30-second rounds, and by round 15 the room is checked out; the middle drags badly enough to sour the whole thing.
  + Chained by design — collect.responses becomes the quiz itself, the exact moat move from COMPARATIVE-ADVANTAGE.md, and each round opens with '{{playerName}} asks:' which is an addressed moment (your question, projected, with your name)
  + The 4-field collect (question + correct + two decoys) is a clear, low-floor authoring task any 14-year-old can do in 90 seconds, and shuffle:true keeps question order unpredictable
  + Everyone gets a guaranteed spotlight moment — every student's question is played, which is the belonging upside of the very thing that makes it drag
  [MAJOR] quiz phase (foreach): iterating over ALL of collect.responses means round count equals class size — 25 students is ~13 minutes of identical show/guess/reveal loops. Cap the foreach at ~10-12 shuffled questions (or add a limit/sample option), or split it into two sittings; the shuffle already makes a sample feel fair.
  [MAJOR] quiz.reveal sub-phase: the per-round payoff is just the correct answer on screen for 6 seconds. No guess distribution, no 'only 3 of you knew this,' no beat for the author to react ('wait, you thought I liked country music?'). That reaction moment is the entire point of the game — show how the class voted alongside the answer, or lengthen/host-pace the reveal so the author can say something.
  [MINOR] quiz.show sub-phase: 4 seconds to read '{{playerName}} asks: {{question}}' aloud is too short — the teacher can't even finish saying the name before it advances. 8-10 seconds, or host-paced.
  [MINOR] collect phase: 90 seconds to invent a question plus write three plausible answers is tight for slower writers, and a weak decoy ('false answer: asdf') poisons that round for everyone. Bump to 150-180s and add one line of guidance in the prompt: 'Make your false answers believable!'
  [MINOR] instructions phase: the message promises '100 points for each question you answer correctly' and ends in leaderboard + winner — fine as play, but the winner is just 'who already knew the most about their friends,' which slightly cuts against the connect tag. Consider ending on the leaderboard without the winner crowning, or reframe the end message around what was learned rather than who won.

## corn-story — FINE — worth 3/5 — ~20min
A coherent, high-energy three-round elimination with real structural cleverness: AI semantic matching makes round 1's 'don't match' actually adjudicable, and eliminated players becoming the final jury keeps everyone in the room. Solid library keeper, but the near-duplicate round 2/3 prompts and a pure competition shape keep it off the front page.
  + Round 1's AI compare task is genuinely load-bearing — instantly grouping 'eat it' / 'eating corn' across 25 answers is something no teacher could do live and no competitor can do at all
  + Eliminated players vote in round 2 (voters: all) and exclusively decide the final (voters: eliminated) — elimination never means spectating, which is the game's best design decision
  + round1-show reveals everyone's answers BEFORE the AI verdict — a real scan-the-wall suspense beat
  + Escalating stakes arc (luck-ish matching → peer-judged creativity → jury verdict) builds correctly to the crown
  [MAJOR] round2-collect ('alternative use for corn') and final-collect ('your ultimate creative use for corn') are the same question asked twice — a finalist who spent their best idea in round 2 has nothing left for the final, and the class hears recycled answers at the climax. Keep it in the library as the vocab-review template it claims to be; it earns its minutes only after a teacher swaps in real unit content.
  + Intro framing 'fastest fingers don't matter, correct ones do' directly counters Kahoot-style speed pressure and matches the pedagogy doc's low-floor commitment
  + Match-phase close is a genuine whole-class beat: per-pair accuracy bars turn each round into a short discussion moment rather than a silent score dump
  + The arc is tight and legible — intro, two rounds, leaderboard, done; nothing drags and the two-round leaderboard sum is easy to explain aloud
  [MAJOR] round1 (French animal words) and round2 (inventors) are two unrelated subjects, so the scoreboard leaderboard sums knowledge of nothing in particular — no teacher can run this unedited, and a first-time visitor trying it experiences an incoherent 'class' with no class. Ship a single-subject default (e.g. both rounds French, or both rounds inventors) so the demo itself makes sense end-to-end.
  [MINOR] Only 9 pairs total at 10 points each (90 max) means heavy score ties in a 25-student class — the leaderboard payoff lands flat when eight students share first place. Add a pair or two per round, or note in the template copy that more pairs sharpen the leaderboard.
  [MINOR] The end phase message 'Swap in your own pairs for any unit review' addresses the TEACHER but is projected to students on the host screen — students just read instructions meant for someone else. Move the swap-your-pairs pitch to the library card/description and give students a real closer.
  [MINOR] round1's 60s timer for 5 easy pairs means fast students finish in ~15s and sit idle for 45s with nothing to do — either shorten to 40s or rely on the all-submitted auto-close and say so in the intro ('round ends when everyone's in').
